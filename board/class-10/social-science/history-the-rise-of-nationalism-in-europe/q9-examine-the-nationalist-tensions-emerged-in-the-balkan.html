Examine the nationalist tensions emerged in the Balkan region by the end of nineteenth century.

CBSE 2026QP 32/5/1Q75 marks
Question 9 · Class 10 Social Science · History: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
Question as text
(a) Examine the nationalist tensions emerged in the Balkan region by the end of nineteenth century. OR (b) Examine the role of Giuseppe Mazzini in the unification of Italy during the nineteenth century.
Answer
Answer as text
(a) (i) The Balkans was a region of geographical and ethnic variation (modern Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia, Montenegro), whose inhabitants were broadly known as Slavs. (ii) A large part of the Balkans was under the Ottoman Empire; the spread of romantic nationalism along with the disintegration of the Ottoman Empire made the region very explosive. (iii) One by one, its European subject nationalities broke away and declared independence, using history to prove that they had once been independent. (iv) The Balkan states were fiercely jealous of each other and each hoped to gain more territory at the expense of the others. (v) The big European powers (Russia, Germany, England, Austro-Hungary) competed for control over the Balkans for trade, colonies and military might; this led to a series of wars and finally the First World War (1914). OR (b) (i) Giuseppe Mazzini, born in Genoa in 1807, became a member of the secret society of the Carbonari. (ii) At 24 he was sent into exile in 1831 for attempting a revolution in Liguria. (iii) He founded the secret societies Young Italy in Marseilles and Young Europe in Berne, whose members were like-minded young men from Poland, France, Italy and the German states. (iv) He believed that God had intended nations to be the natural units of mankind, so Italy could not remain a patchwork of small states and had to be forged into a single unified republic. (v) He sought to put together a coherent programme for a unitary Italian Republic; though the uprisings of 1831 and 1848 failed, his ideas inspired others, and Metternich called him “the most dangerous enemy of our social order”.
